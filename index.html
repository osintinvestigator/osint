Genealogy research

Most of the work: tracing families back through records kept in Poland and the lands of the former partitions.

  • Ancestor search in Polish archives

    Births, marriages and deaths from parish and civil registers, State Archives, diocesan archives and the online indexes built on them.

  • Emigration tracing

    Passenger lists, naturalisation papers and church records abroad, used to find the exact village or parish your family left.

  • Reading old records

    Handwritten entries in Polish, Latin, Russian and German, transcribed and translated into plain English.

  • 20th-century fates

    What happened to relatives during the wars, deportations and border changes, from military, displacement and emigration-era sources.

  • Living relatives

    Locating adult descendants of a shared ancestor, using lawful open sources and a careful, consent-minded approach to first contact.

  • Checking an existing tree

    A second look at research you or someone else has done: which links are proven, which are guesses, and what to search next.

How the research works

Everything happens by email, so you keep a written record of every question and every answer.

  1. You write to me

    Names, approximate dates, places, family stories and any documents you already have, even partial or uncertain ones.

  2. I assess what is possible

    Which records are likely to survive, where they are held, and a proposed scope and estimate before any work starts.

  3. Research

    Archives, indexes and open sources are searched methodically. You get short updates when something significant turns up.

  4. Your report

    A family tree and a written report in English or Polish, with each finding cited, record copies where available, and a list of what was searched without result.

Questions

How far back can Polish records go?

Many Catholic parish registers survive from the 17th or 18th century, and civil-religious registers were kept in most of central Poland from 1808. Wars destroyed some collections, so how far a line can be taken depends on the parish. I tell you what is likely to survive before any work begins.

Do I need to know the village my family came from?

No. Finding the place of origin is often the first part of the job. Emigration, naturalisation, military and church records in the destination country frequently name the town or parish.

My family's surname was changed after emigration. Can you still find them?

Usually, yes. Anglicised and misspelled names are common; I work from the Polish, Latin, German or Russian forms that would have appeared in the original records.

Can we talk by phone or video call?

No. All communication is by email. It keeps a written record of the scope, the questions and the findings, and it lets you reply in your own time.

What will I receive at the end?

A written report in English or Polish: the family tree, every finding linked to its source, copies or links to records where available, and a clear list of what was searched and not found.
